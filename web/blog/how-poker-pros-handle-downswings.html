How Poker Pros Handle Downswings: What Negreanu, Ivey and Adelstein’s Results Show

By Rohit Kaul · September 15, 2026 · 15 min read

Garrett Adelstein's memoir, Beneath the Cards, arrived on September 1 from Permuted Press, with Simon & Schuster distributing. Its author is the player the publisher calls the best high-stakes cash game player on earth. By the publisher's own framing, the book is about what happens once winning stops being enough (Permuted Press).

Every pro's graph has a hole in it. Negreanu's is $2.2 million deep. Ivey's online hole runs past $6 million on a single screen name. The difference between those players and the reg quietly reloading at $2/$5 isn't the size of the hole. It's that they can measure it, and they know what a hole that size is supposed to look like.

TL;DR: A poker downswing is measured in buy-ins, not dollars, against the spread your edge and field size allow. Negreanu's 2023 was roughly −74 buy-ins over 145 events; whether that was luck depends entirely on the per-event standard deviation you assume, and one year can't settle it. Ivey's tracked online loss reached $6.3 million on one account while he won $1.7 million in tournaments the same year (CardPlayer). The practical response: move down by the bankroll math, cut volume before strategy, and keep logging every session so the diagnosis is yours and not a feeling.

What is a poker downswing, really?

A downswing is a run of results well below your expected value, counted in buy-ins for tournaments and buy-ins or big blinds for cash, never dollars. How far below is normal? Primedope's tournament calculator puts the 95% confidence interval for a 20% ROI player at 500 tournaments at roughly −140% to +180% ROI (Primedope). That whole band is variance.

Units matter because dollars don't scale. A $50,000 losing month is twenty buy-ins at $2,500 and five at $10,000: different diagnoses. Counting in buy-ins puts the result on the same axis as your edge, so you can ask whether it sits inside the distribution or outside it.

Two inputs shape that distribution: your edge, and the spread around it. Field size drives the spread. GTO Wizard simulated a 10% ROI player over 1,000 tournaments at $50 a seat. In 1,000-runner fields that player is down after all 1,000 events 40% of the time; in 100-runner fields, 18% (GTO Wizard). Same edge, same volume, more than double the chance of a losing sample.

So the question has two halves: is the result below expectation, and is it further below than the spread allows? Our bankroll guide covers what the spread means for how many buy-ins to hold; the tournament variance post ran the second question on a generic schedule. Here we run it on three real ledgers.

How bad was Negreanu's 2023, and was it variance?

Negreanu lost $2,228,174 across 145 tournaments in 2023, the worst year of his career, according to the year-end vlog he published and PokerNews reported (PokerNews). Whether that was variance depends on the per-event standard deviation you assume, and a single year can't settle it. His own diagnosis: he was "playing far too often."

He's rare in publishing a ledger at all. His site lists 2013 to 2017 with events, buy-ins and payouts: +$1.96 million, +$7.1 million, +$953,000, −$1.25 million and −$86,000, for +$8.73 million on $10.3 million of buy-ins across 291 events (danielnegreanu.com). Two losing years in five, and the 2016 loss came two years after a $7.1 million year.

From 2018 the figures come from press quoting his tweets and vlogs rather than a public table, so treat them as self-reported. 2018 and 2019 were +$1.41 million and +$832,000 (poker.org); 2020 was nothing. 2021 was +$584,000 on $2.6 million of buy-ins (HighstakesDB). 2022 was +$1.63 million on $3.22 million, for a ten-year total of $13.1 million, per the tweet CardPlayer quoted (CardPlayer).

Then 2023: 145 events, 39 cashes, a 26.9% cash rate (PokerStrategy), and a $2.23 million loss. The cash rate was fine. The money wasn't. PokerNews' reading of the vlog: his deep runs came in lower buy-in, massive-field events and never covered the non-cashes at $25,000 and up. That's a field-size story, which is why the buy-in conversion matters.

So convert it. He hasn't published a 2023 average buy-in, so we use his 2022 average of $30,136 as a stated proxy, which makes −$2,228,174 about −74 buy-ins over 145 events. We ran that schedule through the same deterministic model as our variance calculator. It's a normal model: per-event mean equal to ROI in buy-ins, standard deviation in buy-ins, drawdowns from a 20,000-trial seeded Monte Carlo. No AI touched it and nothing was fitted; the only inputs are the four ROI and SD pairs below.

Our read: whether 2023 "was variance" depends entirely on the standard deviation you believe in. In a tight small-field high-roller model, 3 buy-ins per event, a −74 buy-in year is a 1-in-150 to 1-in-500 event; something beyond luck was going on. In a big-field, re-entry model at 6 buy-ins per event it's roughly a 1-in-10 year, unremarkable. One year cannot tell those two worlds apart. That's the lesson, not the number.

Negreanu's own verdict lands on the first reading: too much volume, sloppiness after winning too much, and an end to the "shrug, gamble, and rebuy" approach for 2024 (poker.org). He could put a number on the year because he'd logged every buy-in and every cash for a decade. Without the ledger, 2023 is a bad feeling. With it, 2023 is 74 buy-ins against a known schedule.

One cross-check from outside our model. PokerStrategy ran Primedope on a super-high-roller schedule close to his ($30,000 average buy-in, 80 runners, 9% ROI, 6% rake, 180 events a year) and got a losing year 39% of the time (PokerStrategy). A losing year on that schedule is routine. A 74-buy-in one is not, unless the fields are far bigger than 80.

Did Phil Ivey really lose $6 million online?

Yes, on one screen name, and tracked hand by hand. HighstakesDB's data, as reported by CardPlayer, has Ivey's "Polarizing" account on Full Tilt down $6.3 million from late 2012 to December 2015 (CardPlayer). His original account there had won $19.24 million over about 319,000 hands. Across both his accounts, 2015 alone cost $3.73 million.

The upswing was real and it was long. HighstakesDB's year-by-year for the original "Phil Ivey" account runs from $2.0 million in 2007 to a $7.0 million peak in 2008, then $6.0 million and $2.2 million (HighstakesDB). Then Black Friday, a new screen name, and a very different graph.

By August 2014 "Polarizing" was down $5.1 million, which CardPlayer noted made it the fifth-worst account in Full Tilt's history (CardPlayer). 2015 made it worse. That account dropped a further $1.25 million, while his "RaiseOnce" account on PokerStars lost $2,481,266 over 126,026 hands and 622 sessions by PokerNewsDaily's count (PokerNewsDaily). Combined: −$3.73 million over roughly 160,000 hands. Inside that year sits $1,047,781 lost in a single week in June (PokerStrategy).

Two things about that graph. It didn't erase him: as of May 2018 HighstakesDB still had his lifetime online result at +$10.44 million, describing the post-Full-Tilt stretch as an $8 million downswing (HighstakesDB). And in the same year his online accounts lost $3.73 million, he won $1.7 million in tournaments (CardPlayer). A downswing on one axis says nothing about the others.

Our read: nobody re-rated Ivey's skill off that graph, rightly. Sample and stakes did the damage: 160,000 hands is a big sample for a live player and a modest one at stakes where a single week can move seven figures. In units of his own edge and spread, it's a documented, survivable hole. The win-rate post covers why the hands, not the dollars, decide how much a graph can tell you.

What does Beneath the Cards say about winning?

Beneath the Cards came out September 1 2026 from Permuted Press, in paperback, Kindle and an 11-hour audiobook read by Adelstein (PokerNews). In the publisher's own framing it's a memoir about what happens once winning stops being enough (garrettadelstein.com). The Jack-Four hand takes up about 10 percent of it, by Adelstein's own estimate, per the PokerNews review.

For our readers this is the relevant one of the three. Negreanu and Ivey are tournament and nosebleed-online stories; Adelstein's game, live cash, is the one that looks like yours, scaled up. The publisher's description promises drunken binges, death threats, financial ruin and public backlash alongside the poker. somuchpoker's write-up of Galfond's endorsement talks of $3 million swings in a single session and the reality of going broke (somuchpoker).

The blurbs come from people who'd know. Galfond says it captures the high-stakes world better than anything he's read; Mike Brady's line is that it shows the astronomical highs and the crushing lows (garrettadelstein.com). PokerNews' reviewer, Chad Holloway, warns that readers hoping for a Jack-Four smoking gun won't get one, and still says it belongs on a poker player's shelf.

The hand: on September 29 2022 on Hustler Casino Live, Adelstein's eight-high draw lost a $105,000 all-in pot to Robbi Jade Lew's jack-four. The investigation HCL commissioned reported in December 2022 that it "found no conclusive evidence of wrongdoing" related to the hand (Hustler Casino Live; PokerNews). The book's own line on it, quoted by Jennifer Shahade: "After a lifetime of running good, I can finally see that fateful day for what it was: I got really fucking unlucky." (Deep Reads)

The downswing angle is the perfectionism. Adelstein tells poker.org he's a "recovering perfectionist" who quit anything he couldn't be the best at (poker.org). That's the trait that produces a great win rate and a terrible relationship with variance, and it's why the book belongs in a post about downswings.

How do you know if you're in a downswing or just playing badly?

Mostly you can't, not from results, and the numbers on that are blunt. Phil Galfond's figures for a cash player at 25,000 hands a year: a 13% chance of a losing year with a 5bb/100 edge, 26.4% at 2bb/100 (Galfond). Primedope: remove your top five results from 1,000 tournaments and you may be break-even or negative (Primedope).

Results are a noisy instrument for measuring play. Negreanu's 2023 cash rate was 26.9%, up from 21.5% in 2022 (PokerStrategy, CardPlayer), and it was his worst year by more than $2 million. What actually works is four dull habits.

  • Know your sample before you name the swing. Primedope's 500-tournament band runs from −140% to +180% ROI for a 20% ROI player. A 200-tournament sample can describe a downswing; it can't diagnose one.
  • Keep expenses out of the result. Travel, tips and food belong in a separate column, so a bad month on the road doesn't read as a bad month at the table.
  • Compare against your own logged rate, not a feeling. The question is how far this stretch sits below your tracked win rate, hours attached, not below the rate you'd like to have. The win-rate post is about why that number needs its sample stated.
  • Review hands, not results. Negreanu's diagnosis came from reviewing his schedule; yours comes from the hands. Record the ones where you weren't sure, and revisit them when you're not tilted. Logging sessions and hands in one place is what makes that review possible a month later.

None of the four tells you the answer. They make it checkable: where this stretch sits against the model's median drawdown, and whether the month's biggest pots were correct decisions. Without the log, all you can say is that it feels bad.

What should you actually change during a downswing?

Less than instinct says, and almost none of it is strategy. GTO Wizard's bankroll guidance is 75 to 125 buy-ins for tournaments, never more than 2% of your roll in a single event, and a warning that the 100-buy-in rule is far too aggressive for most large-field MTTs (GTO Wizard). The first lever is the stake.

  • Move down when the roll says so, not when the mood does. PokerStrategy notes that variance calculators don't account for moving up or down in stakes (PokerStrategy); that's the point. Moving down is the one lever that changes the risk-of-ruin math the calculators can't see.
  • Cut volume before you cut strategy. Negreanu's 2024 plan was fewer events, no shrug-gamble-rebuy, and no WSOP Player of the Year chase (PokerNews). His summers since: 2024 still lost $171,000 at the WSOP (CardPlayer). 2025 was 45 events, 15 cashes and +$181,097, his first profitable series since 2021 (PokerNews). 2026 came in at +$1.69 million (CardPlayer). Three data points prove nothing; what they show is a player adjusting the inputs he controls.
  • Don't try to reduce variance. Galfond's line: "If your goal is to reduce your swings, you don't need to reduce your variance. You need to get better at poker." (Galfond) The spread is the price of the edge, and the edge is the only part you own. A specific leak beats a vague resolution; the calling-stations post is one concrete place to start.
  • Keep the log honest through the hole. The temptation is to stop entering losing sessions until the graph looks better. Negreanu could diagnose 2023 because the log ran straight through it. Galfond was tweeting through the losing start of the 2020 Galfond Challenge, telling worried followers not to be (PokerStrategy).
  • Borrow the mindset lines that aren't fluff. Ryan Fee: "A lot of people treat poker like a sprint, when it's really a marathon." Matt Waxman's version: you can't control one tournament's outcome, so focus on what you can (CardPlayer).

Then put your own logged ROI and standard deviation into the variance calculator and look at the median drawdown for your volume. If your current hole sits inside it, the plan is the stake and the volume. If it sits well outside, the plan is the hands.

FAQ

How long does a poker downswing last?

There's no fixed length; the honest measure is how often a winning player posts a losing stretch. Galfond's figures for a cash player at 25,000 hands a year: a 13% chance of a losing year at 5bb/100, 26.4% at 2bb/100 (Galfond). For a super-high-roller schedule modelled on Primedope, a winning player has a 15% chance of losing money over a full decade of 1,800 tournaments (PokerStrategy).

What is a normal downswing in buy-ins for tournaments?

It depends on field size more than on skill. In our deterministic model of a 145-event year, the median worst drawdown for a 10–20% ROI player runs from 29–33 buy-ins in a tight small-field model (3 buy-ins of standard deviation per event) to 66–71 buy-ins in a big-field, re-entry model (6 buy-ins). Primedope puts the 95% confidence interval for a 20% ROI player at 500 tournaments at roughly −140% to +180% ROI (Primedope).

How much did Phil Ivey lose online?

By HighstakesDB's tracking as reported by CardPlayer, Ivey's "Polarizing" account on Full Tilt was down $6.3 million from late 2012 to December 2015, after his original account there had won $19.24 million over about 319,000 hands (CardPlayer). In 2015 alone he lost $3.73 million across Full Tilt and PokerStars over roughly 160,000 hands, while winning $1.7 million in tournaments. As of May 2018 HighstakesDB still had his lifetime online result at +$10.44 million.

What was Daniel Negreanu's worst year?

2023. By his own year-end vlog, he lost $2,228,174 across 145 tournaments with 39 cashes, the biggest losing year of his career (PokerNews). At his 2022 average buy-in of $30,136 that's roughly 74 buy-ins. His own diagnosis was that he was playing far too often; his 2024 plan cut volume and dropped the WSOP Player of the Year chase.

Is Beneath the Cards about the Jack-Four hand?

Only partly. Adelstein told PokerNews the alleged cheating incident makes up about 10 percent of the book, and the PokerNews review warns that readers looking for a Jack-Four smoking gun won't find one (PokerNews). Jennifer Shahade's review notes the hand still occupies a substantial portion. The rest is a memoir of a high-stakes live cash career: the swings, the anxiety, going broke, and what happens when winning is no longer enough.

Should you move down in stakes during a downswing?

If the bankroll math says so, yes, and the math is the only good reason. GTO Wizard's guidance is 75–125 buy-ins for tournaments and never more than 2% of your roll in a single event, with a warning that the 100-buy-in rule is far too aggressive for most large-field MTTs (GTO Wizard). PokerStrategy notes that variance calculators don't account for moving up or down in stakes, which is exactly why moving down is the one lever that changes your risk of ruin.

The bottom line

Every graph in this post has a hole in it, and each of the three players could tell you how deep, in buy-ins, against a known schedule. That's the real difference between a pro's downswing and everyone else's. Not size, not stoicism: measurement, and the honesty to say when the number is inside the distribution.

The math is the same at $2/$5 as at $30,000 a seat: buy-ins, not dollars; spread, not feeling; hands, not results. Poker carries real variance and real risk, and nothing here is financial advice. Start logging every session and every hand that bothered you, and the next time your graph dips you'll know what you're looking at.
