The EPT Cut Its Buy-In and WSOP Paradise Cut Its Guarantee. Where Does a Tournament Buy-In Go?

By Rohit Kaul · November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

PokerStars has trimmed the price of a European Poker Tour Main Event seat for the first time in more than fifteen years. From 2027 it costs €5,000, down from €5,300 (PokerNews). In a little over two weeks, WSOP Paradise opens in the Bahamas with a smaller promise on its biggest tournament: a $50 million guarantee, where last year's poster said $60 million (WSOP.com; PokerNews).

One tour made the seat cheaper. The other promised less. Both are headlines about price, and neither tells you the thing a tournament player most needs to know, which is how much of the money you hand over actually gets played for. That number is rarely on the poster, and it varies a lot more than most players realise.

TL;DR: The EPT Main Event drops to €5,000 next year, and WSOP Paradise has lowered its Super Main Event guarantee to $50 million. A cheaper seat is only a better deal if the saving comes out of the fees, and PokerStars hasn't yet said whether it does. The Paradise guarantee is unlikely to matter, because last year's field beat it comfortably. The bigger story for most players is rake: a small buy-in gives up around a sixth of itself before a card is dealt, while a high roller gives up almost nothing. Know that share before you register, and measure your results against everything you paid.

What did PokerStars change for 2027?

The Main Event price, and the tour's second-tier event along with it. Every EPT Main Event in 2027 costs €5,000. This December's stop in Prague is the last one at the old price (TightPoker).

The PokerStars Open, the smaller main event that runs alongside, moves to a single price of €1,250 wherever it is held. That makes it noticeably cheaper at the big EPT stops and a little more expensive at the regional festivals, where it used to cost less. Cedric Billot, who runs live events for PokerStars, said players had told the company the Open had become too expensive at EPT stops, and that round, consistent prices make the tour easier to follow (PokerNews).

Is a cheaper seat a better deal?

Only if the saving comes out of the fees, and we don't know that yet. It is natural to read €5,300 as a €5,000 tournament with a €300 fee on top, and to assume the fee is what just disappeared. The real split is less tidy. PokerNews published the breakdown for Prague, and about €450 of each entry never reaches the prize pool. Part of it is the house fee and part is a charge for staff. Call it 8.5% of what you pay.

PokerStars has not published the breakdown for the new price. If the €300 came off the fees, players are getting a real improvement, and a rare one. If it came off the prize pool instead, you pay a little less and play for a little less, and the deal is the same as before. Your bankroll stretches further either way, which is worth something. Whether the tournament itself got better value is still an open question.

Our read: wait for the first 2027 festival schedule, which will carry the fee breakdown. The headline price moved. Whether the value moved depends on a line that hasn't been printed yet.

What changed at WSOP Paradise?

Almost everything except the dates. The series runs December 2 to 17, moves from Atlantis to Baha Mar, and grows to twenty bracelet events (PokerNews). The $25,000 Super Main Event is still the centrepiece, and its guarantee is the one number that went down.

That sounds like a retreat, and in practice it probably changes nothing. A guarantee is a floor. It only matters if too few players show up, in which case the organizer makes up the difference. Last year's Super Main beat its guarantee by a wide margin, and the field would have to shrink by about a third before this year's lower figure came into play. The WSOP hasn't given a reason for the change, and we won't guess at one.

One practical note if you are going. The WSOP's own schedule lists the Super Main at a slightly higher price than the $25K on the banner, and says a percentage of buy-ins is held back for staff and running costs. Neither is unusual. Both are worth a question at the registration desk, so you know what you are paying and what you are playing for.

How much of a poker tournament buy-in is rake?

Anywhere from about a twenty-fifth of it to about a sixth, and the cheaper the tournament, the bigger the bite. Rake here means everything that doesn't reach the prize pool: the house's fee plus whatever is held back for dealers and staff. Card Player compared the same events across the last decade, and the pattern is consistent from tour to tour (Card Player).

The slope makes sense. A dealer costs the same whether the players at the table paid $500 or $100,000, so the cost of running a tournament weighs far more heavily on a small entry. The trend over time points the same way. In Card Player's comparison the cut has grown at the low buy-ins over the past ten years, while the biggest events have held steady and one or two have even come down.

A few percentage points matter more than they look, because rake is a head start you give the house before the first hand. In a high roller you only have to be slightly better than the field to get your money back over time. In the cheapest events on a schedule you have to be roughly a fifth better than the average player just to break even, and better again to show a profit.

The fields in those cheap events are also the softest, and that may well cover the difference. But the tournament with the lowest price is usually the one with the highest cost, and it helps to know that going in.

What does this mean for your bankroll?

It means treating the price of a tournament as what you actually paid, and judging your results against that.

  • Log the full amount. If you handed over €5,300, your return should be measured on €5,300, fees included. Leave the fee out and your results look better than they are. Keep flights, hotels and food on a separate line, so you can see both how you played and what the trip cost.
  • Count every re-entry. A second bullet is a second buy-in, with a second helping of rake. A cheaper seat makes each one hurt a little less. It does nothing for the decision to fire it.
  • Plan for the swings, whatever the guarantee says. A big guarantee tells you the prize pool will be large. It says nothing about how often you will cash. In fields of thousands, long losing stretches are normal even for winning players, and you can try your own numbers in the variance calculator before you book.

What to watch next

Two things. The fee breakdown on the first 2027 EPT schedule will show where the €300 went. And the Super Main Event starts on December 10, so we will soon know whether the lower guarantee was ever in danger. We will update this post when WSOP Paradise finishes.

FAQ

How much is the EPT Main Event buy-in in 2027?

The European Poker Tour Main Event costs €5,000 from the 2027 season, down from €5,300. It is the first change to that price in more than fifteen years. EPT Prague in December 2026 is the last stop at the old price (PokerNews; TightPoker).

How much of a poker tournament buy-in is rake?

It depends on the size of the buy-in. A $500 event gives up around a sixth of each entry to fees and staff costs, a mid-priced event such as the EPT Main Event gives up about 8.5%, and a $100,000 high roller only about 4%. The cheaper the tournament, the larger the share that never reaches the prize pool.

What is the WSOP Paradise 2026 Super Main Event guarantee?

The Super Main Event guarantees $50 million in 2026, down from $60 million in 2025. Last year the field beat its guarantee by a wide margin, so the lower figure is unlikely to change what the winners are paid (WSOP.com; PokerNews).

Does a tournament guarantee mean the prize pool will be that big?

A guarantee is a floor. If too few players enter, the organizer adds the difference, which is called an overlay and is good news for everyone in the field. If enough players enter, the guarantee has no effect and the prize pool is simply what the entries add up to.

The bottom line

The EPT made its seat cheaper and hasn't yet said which part of the price got smaller. WSOP Paradise promised less on a tournament that should sail past the promise anyway. Neither headline is the number to watch. Before you register for anything, find out how much of your entry is actually played for, compare that across the events you are choosing between, and keep an honest record of what you paid.
